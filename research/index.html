You do not need a university post to do this work

SAIN’s offer is one line: bring your expertise to AI Safety, and the community, supervision, and support are here to take it further. The rest of this page is the mechanism behind that line: how a project is chosen, what support looks like week to week, and where the work ends up.

The hub is open to anyone with sufficient background:

  • Students at Bachelor, Master, or PhD level
  • Researchers, and people working in industry
  • Policy professionals
  • People with no formal affiliation

Remote participation works. Applications are rolling, so you can apply whenever you are ready, and for supervised projects you hear back within a working week.

One thing the hub is not: a job. SAIN does not pay stipends or salaries; participation is voluntary and educational. What SAIN does provide is supervision, compute, coordination, and, within reason, help with conference travel.

From open question to published finding

Every project in the hub moves through the same three steps. The handbook spells each one out in full; this is the shape of it.

  1. 01

    Choose a project

    There are three ways in, and all three are normal.

    1. 1

      Join a supervisor's project. Every project a SAIN supervisor has offered is listed at the top of this page. Find one that fits your background and tell the projects team you are interested; they introduce you to the supervisor.

    2. 2

      Join an open collaboration. Some projects run without formal supervision. One person leads, usually whoever designed the project, and SAIN connects collaborators and supports the work.

    3. 3

      Bring your own idea. Write a short proposal: the question, why it matters, how you would test it, and what you need. SAIN's Project Team reviews it, and can help you shape it if it is not fully formed yet.

    Not every valuable contribution is a research paper: the work can be technical, governance, policy, or conceptual. When choosing projects, the team looks at three things: relevance to the Netherlands and Europe, relevance to transformative AI, and whether it can succeed on a small budget. If you are unsure whether a topic counts as AI Safety, the default answer is yes, provided you can articulate a plausible pathway from the work to reducing risk from transformative AI.

    Read the Project Hub handbook
  2. 02

    Work with support

    Fill in the interest form and, for supervised projects, you hear back within a working week. If there is a fit, a supervisor is formally assigned and the project gets its own channel in the community.

    Supervision is a working relationship, not a lecture. You drive the day-to-day work; your supervisor helps scope the project realistically, reviews experiments and drafts, and steps in when a direction stops being productive. Meetings run weekly or biweekly, and you agree your commitment per project up front, whether that is five to ten hours a week alongside a degree or something closer to full time.

    Supervisors come from universities, industry, and government. In academia they are usually at PhD level or more senior; outside it, they have several years of senior experience in a relevant role. Compute is arranged per project at the start, and computationally inexpensive projects are encouraged. Remote participation works throughout.

    Meet the supervisors
  3. 03

    Publish your findings

    A project is complete when its main question is adequately answered, including when the answer is negative, and the result is published in a presentable form: a paper, a policy brief, an evaluation or open-source tool, or a blog post on SAIN’s Substack or LessWrong. You get feedback on drafts, and SAIN may help with conference travel within reason.

    That is not hypothetical. Work from this community has been accepted at workshops at NeurIPS and ICLR, which is where most first contributions in this field land.

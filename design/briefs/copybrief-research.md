# Research hub copy brief

Page: `/research`. One h1. Zero kickers (the kicker budget is spent on none; every heading below carries its band without help). British spelling throughout. No em dashes, no en-dash separators; hyphens, commas, or two sentences.

## Avatar

**Daan, 24, MSc Artificial Intelligence student in Groningen.** He has done the coursework, read a handful of alignment posts, maybe finished a fellowship or an intro course. He knows what NeurIPS and ICLR are and knows the difference between a main-track paper and a workshop paper, so inflated venue claims would end his trust instantly. He is deciding one thing: is this a credible place to make a first real research contribution, or a Discord server with ambitions? He will commit 5 to 10 hours a week if, and only if, he can see a real process (how projects are chosen, who supervises, what gets published) and real people with checkable affiliations. He bounces on: hype, vague "20+ researchers" stats he cannot trace, jargon like "PhD+" left undefined, and any smell of a portfolio padded with overstated venues.

**Secondary read, not a second page:** Dr. E., an experienced researcher (postdoc or professor) scanning for reputability. The same evidence that convinces Daan (named supervisors with verifiable posts, honest workshop-track papers, a written process) is what moves her. She gets exactly one "Become a supervisor" moment, placed directly after the proof, not a parallel page-within-a-page.

## Premises

In order. Each is a thing the page must establish in the reader's head before the next lands.

1. **This page is about doing research, and I am eligible.** WHY: Daan's first question is "is this for someone like me?" The landing made the offer; this page must immediately confirm that students, industry people, and the unaffiliated all qualify, remotely, for free, or he leaves before the process starts.
2. **There is an actual process, with three steps I can name.** WHY: the current page throws facts at the reader and makes him assemble them. A visible choose - work - publish spine is what separates "credible programme" from "vibes". It is also the honest answer to "what would I actually be signing up for?"
3. **Choosing a project is concrete: agendas, open collaborations, or my own proposal.** WHY: "join our research" is a dead ask without a mechanism. Naming the three doors makes the first action imaginable tonight, not someday.
4. **Support is specific: a named supervisor, an agreed cadence, agreed hours, compute per project.** WHY: the beginner's real fear is being abandoned mid-project; the specific commitments (reply within a working week, weekly or biweekly meetings, commitment agreed per project) answer it with facts, not reassurance.
5. **The output is real and honestly labelled: workshop papers at NeurIPS and ICLR workshops, benchmarks, policy briefs.** WHY: this is the proof the whole page rests on. Saying "workshop" plainly is not a weakness, it is the credibility move: Daan can verify every paper, and Dr. E. reads the honesty as competence.
6. **The supervisors are real people with checkable posts and public agendas.** WHY: people are the credibility. An agenda document a reader can open beats any adjective.
7. **The door is open now: rolling applications, one form, one email.** WHY: the page runs on logos; the close should be the logical conclusion (the process exists, the people exist, therefore apply), not a mood shift.
8. **Everything above is written down in a handbook.** WHY: an organisation that documents its own process in public is the strongest reputability signal this page has; it also serves the reader who wants the full terms before committing.

### 2. The process (the spine: three flowing steps)

**(a) Question answered:** what would actually happen, in order, if I joined?

**(b) Ground:** inverse navy, one band containing all three steps (inverse is earned here: the band ends in the page's proof, the publications). Use `band-research`.

**(c) Copy:**

- **Band heading (heading):** From open question to published finding
- **Band intro (body, white/75):** Every project in the hub moves through the same three steps. The handbook spells each one out in full; this is the shape of it.

**Step 01 (heading-sm): Choose a research project**

- **Body:** There are three ways in, and all three are normal.
- **Numbered list (serif-numeral step-list rows or plain numbered rows within the step):**
  1. **Work from a supervisor's agenda.** Every SAIN supervisor keeps a public research agenda, a document of open questions they want help investigating. Read the agendas below and pick a question that fits your background.
  2. **Join an open collaboration.** Some projects run without formal supervision. One researcher leads, usually the person who designed the project, and SAIN connects collaborators and supports the work.
  3. **Bring your own idea.** Write a short proposal: the question, why it matters, how you would test it, and what you need. SAIN's research team reviews it, and can help you shape it if it is not fully formed yet.
- **Closing body:** The scope is broad on purpose: technical alignment and interpretability, governance and policy with an EU and Dutch emphasis, and foundational questions about how the field measures progress. If you are unsure whether a topic counts as AI Safety, the default answer is yes, provided you can articulate a plausible pathway from the work to reducing AI-related risk.
  - House form, noted 13 September 2026: `AI Safety` is capitalised everywhere on this page, matching the h1, the eligibility paragraph, and the landing's `Your AI Safety career starts here.` This line previously read `AI safety` and was the page's only lowercase instance.
- **Text link:** `Read the research handbook` → /research/handbook

**Step 02 (heading-sm): Work with support**

- **Body, paragraph 1:** Fill in the interest form and, for supervised projects, you hear back within a working week. If there is a fit, a supervisor is formally assigned and the project gets its own channel in the community.
- **Body, paragraph 2:** Supervision is a working relationship, not a lecture. You drive the day-to-day research; your supervisor helps scope the project realistically, reviews experiments and drafts, and steps in when a direction stops being productive. Meetings run weekly or biweekly, and you agree your commitment per project up front, whether that is five to ten hours a week alongside a degree or something closer to full time.
- **Body, paragraph 3:** Supervisors are usually researchers at PhD level or more senior, with exceptions. Compute is arranged per project at the start, and computationally inexpensive projects are encouraged. Remote participation works throughout.
- **Text link:** `Meet the supervisors` → #supervisors

**Step 03 (heading-sm): Publish your findings**

- **Body, paragraph 1:** A project is complete when its main question is adequately answered, including when the answer is negative, and the result is published in a presentable form: a workshop paper, a policy brief, or a blog post on SAIN's Substack or LessWrong. You get feedback on drafts, and SAIN may help with conference travel within reason.
- **Body, paragraph 2 (transition into the proof):** That is not hypothetical. Work from this community has been accepted at workshops at NeurIPS and ICLR, which is where most first contributions in this field land.

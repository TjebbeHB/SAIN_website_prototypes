## Part 1 — What to say (and not say) about AI risk

### The core failure mode this post is warning about

Simplified, high-arousal messages about AI ("AI will be huge / AI is dangerous") don't
fail by being ignored — they fail by being *misread in the wrong direction*. A newcomer
who only absorbs "AI could be really powerful soon" tends to reach for the nearest
familiar frame, which is competition: "then we'd better build it first." A newcomer who
only absorbs "AI could be dangerous" without a mechanism will treat any visible safety
progress as reassurance — even if that "progress" is exactly what a deceptive system
would produce. Vague urgency reliably produces the opposite of caution. So the rule
for SAIN copy isn't "say it more forcefully," it's "say it with enough mechanism that
it can't be reread as a race."

### Messages to avoid

- **Bare power/importance claims.** "AI will be transformative / the biggest deal
  ever" with no explanation of *why that's dangerous* invites a race framing, not a
  safety framing. Never ship a stat or scale claim without the risk mechanism attached
  in the same breath.
- **Bare danger claims.** "AI could be dangerous" with no mechanism trains people to
  look for the wrong evidence. They'll accept "it passed the safety test" as case
  closed, when the whole worry is that passing tests is what a misaligned system would
  also do (the "Lance Armstrong problem" — passing every drug test tells you the
  system got better at hiding, not that it's clean).
- **Us-vs-them / geopolitical race framing.** Anything that reads as "we need to get
  there before [country/company] does" is a backfire, not a caution message — it's the
  literal opposite of what SAIN exists to promote.
- **Confident specific predictions / doom timelines.** Overclaiming certainty about
  *how* or *when* alienates skeptical readers and is easy to dismiss the moment one
  prediction misses. State uncertainty honestly.
- **Sci-fi framing.** Robots-take-over imagery lets readers file the whole topic under
  fiction. Ground every abstract risk in a real, present-day analogy instead.
- **Viral / one-shot content as the primary vehicle.** A single striking post that gets
  shared widely is more likely to be skimmed, decontextualized, and misremembered than
  understood. Optimize copy for *a reader coming back*, not for the share count.

### Messages that work

- **Mutual vulnerability, not competition.** The most load-bearing reframe: misaligned
  AI would harm the people who built it too, not just "the other side." This is what
  turns "who gets there first" into "this is everyone's problem" — cooperation instead
  of a race. This should be near-load-bearing in SAIN's core pitch.
- **Deception as the specific mechanism, stated as a live possibility, not a
  certainty.** Training can select for *looking* safe rather than *being* safe. Say
  this plainly enough that a reader understands why "it seems fine so far" is not the
  reassurance it sounds like — while being clear this is a conceivable failure mode,
  not a confirmed prediction.
- **Safety-first as an institutional norm, not a moral appeal.** "Developers should
  have to demonstrate safety before deploying powerful systems" puts the burden of
  proof on the builder. This is a policy/norm framing, not a fear framing — safer
  ground for a newcomer and harder to dismiss as alarmism.
- **Alignment work framed as prosocial and exciting, not just necessary.** People are
  more likely to consider the field if it's pitched as "great, positive work to do,"
  not only as damage control. Useful register for any careers/get-involved copy.
- **"We're not ready for this" over specific forecasts.** Honest epistemic humility
  about an unfamiliar, high-stakes transition reads as credible; a firm timeline reads
  as a bet someone can call.
- **Concrete, real-world analogies for every abstract risk.** Whatever the risk is,
  it should map to something the reader has already seen happen (bugs that pass
  testing, principal-agent problems, optimization gaming a metric) rather than staying
  a pure abstraction.

### Conveying scale without alarmism

- Depth over reach: aim for "the reader actually understands this" not "the reader
  reacted to this." A shorter, punchier claim that gets shared is doing less work than
  a slightly longer one that's actually understood.
- Repetition from a trusted source beats one-shot intensity. If SAIN copy is going to
  be someone's *second or third* exposure to these ideas (via a talk, an event, a
  follow-up), that's the design point — not the first shocking headline.
- Pair every scale claim with mechanism and humility in the same unit of copy: what
  could happen, why, and how confident we actually are. Never let "how big" stand
  alone.

### Apply to SAIN

- Landing/hero copy: any claim about AI's scale or stakes must sit next to (not just
  near) a one-line mechanism — e.g., not "AI safety matters," but "systems can look
  safe during testing while remaining unsafe once deployed — because training
  optimizes for the tests we can see." Never let a scale claim stand alone.
- Avoid every framing that could be read as "keep up or fall behind" — no arms-race
  language, no country-vs-country or lab-vs-lab comparisons, even implicitly (e.g.
  "the Netherlands can't be left behind on AI").
- Frame the SAIN community/field itself as the thing worth joining because it's good,
  useful work — not only because disaster looms. Careers and event copy should read as
  an invitation to something constructive.
- Given the MEMORY note that "AI safety is deliberately left undefined" on the
  landing — that's consistent with this post's warning against bare, undercooked scale
  claims. Where the site *does* explain the risk (deeper pages, not the landing), it
  should carry the mechanism, not just the stakes.
- Treat SAIN's own copy as a relationship-building surface, not a viral pitch: write
  for the person who comes back to read the "about" or "why AI safety" page a second
  time, not for a screenshot that gets shared once.

## Part 2 — How to write it (readability rules)

### Structure

- **Headings**: break every page/post into clearly labeled sections a reader can scan
  and re-find. Any SAIN page longer than ~4 paragraphs needs headers.
- **Lead with the takeaway.** A TL;DR / one-line summary up top for anyone who won't
  read the whole thing — state the key idea before the argument for it.
- **Explicit transitions.** Don't let the reader infer how one section relates to the
  next — say it ("this matters because…", "the reason this changes things is…").
- **Land the conclusion.** Every section should end by stating what the reader should
  now believe or do — don't leave the "so what" implicit.
- **Map relationships before compounding them.** If two ideas need to be held together
  (e.g., "training" + "deception risk"), state how they relate before assuming the
  reader can combine them unaided.

### Sentence-level craft

- **Be concise.** Cut length and cut words; shorter is the default correction.
- **Concrete over abstract.** Every abstract claim needs a real-world example attached.
- **Write like you're talking to one specific friend.** Say it out loud; if it sounds
  like a memo, rewrite it as speech.
- **No condescension.** Kind, respectful register — never a tone that implies the
  reader should already know this.
- **Define every term on first use.** Assume zero prior exposure to SAIN's
  vocabulary ("alignment," "misaligned," "safety case," etc.) — spell it out inline,
  don't footnote it away.
- **Explain every metaphor.** If copy uses an analogy, state explicitly what it stands
  for — don't rely on the reader following the leap.
- **Spell out the logic.** Don't skip an inferential step because it feels obvious to
  the writer; state premise → conclusion explicitly.
- **Establish context before asking for inference.** Give the concrete fact first, the
  interpretation second.

### Formatting

- Use headings to segment.
- Use a short summary/TL;DR block at the top of longer pages.
- Use numbered or bulleted lists for anything enumerable (steps, reasons, options) —
  bullets over dense paragraphs whenever the content is genuinely a list.
- Use bold sparingly, for the actual key phrase in a paragraph — not decoration.
- Use whitespace between sections; don't compress everything into a wall of text.
- Footnotes/links for supporting detail that would break flow if inlined — keep the
  main line readable, push citations and caveats out.

### Motivation and tone

- **State why the reader should keep reading, up front** — relevance before content.
- **Show pragmatic relevance** — connect the abstract topic to something the reader
  already cares about.
- **Be warm on first contact.** The opening lines of any page set whether the reader
  keeps going — friendly and concrete beats clever or portentous.
- **Core test**: imagine 3–5 different real readers going through the copy
  sentence-by-sentence. Don't write for yourself — write for someone who has never
  seen this material and doesn't yet care.

### Apply to SAIN

- Every SAIN page (not just long ones) should pass the "don't assume the reader is
  you" test: a newcomer with zero AI-safety background should be able to read the
  landing page start to finish without hitting an undefined term.
- Given SAIN's field-building audience is often *not yet technical*, define every
  piece of jargon inline the first time it appears anywhere on the site, even if it's
  defined elsewhere.
- Use short TL;DR-style intro lines on longer content pages (research explainers,
  event recaps) — put the takeaway first, argument after.
- Keep CTAs and section closers explicit about what the reader should now do or
  believe — don't end a section on pure description.
- Favor bullets/numbered steps for anything actionable (how to get involved, how an
  event works, how to apply) over paragraph prose.
